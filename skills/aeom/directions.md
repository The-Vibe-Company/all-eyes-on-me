# Directions

Six directions for one product, and not one of them may look like an AI default.

## The banned looks

A model asked to "be bold" reaches for these. A direction that one of them describes as a whole is out, whatever its quality: screening judges remove it before the bracket, so two banned directions can never meet in a duel. One trait alone does not make a banned look; a cream background or a serif headline can be a real brand choice. It is the combination that gives the default away.

- Cream or newsprint paper, a big serif headline, a red, brick or terracotta accent.
- A near-black screen with one amber, acid green or phosphor accent, in monospace.
- Newspaper hairlines, magazine columns, drop caps, pull quotes.
- A white Swiss poster: a huge grotesque headline and one red square.
- A black-and-yellow brutalist grid with heavy borders.
- Didot or another high-contrast serif with small caps and a gold or brass accent.
- Kraft paper, rubber stamps, scalloped edges, hand-drawn labels.
- A purple-to-blue gradient, glassy cards, glow.
- Inter or Space Grotesk on white with rounded cards.
- On a game or a creative product, a document interface laid over it: a survey map with contour lines and a graduated frame, an administrative form, a dashboard of cards and charts, a spreadsheet. Players open a game to enter its world, not to read a document about it.

Design movements are banned as starting points for the same reason: "Swiss", "brutalist", "editorial", "retro terminal", "luxury" and "artisanal" are exactly where these looks come from.

## Where a direction comes from

From the product's own world, not from design history. That world is the product brief, `aeom product --brief`: what kind of product it is, what the app is for, who uses it, its main loop, and for a game or a creative product its world. A tool's world is the real one its users live in; a game's is its fiction. The steps below are for a tool; a game or a creative product follows **For a game or a creative product** further down.

1. **List twelve sources** that the people the brief names would recognise, around what the app is for and what they do again and again, and that are not websites: an object they handle, a document they receive, a sign they read, a place they go, a trade that serves them, a tool, a package, a form. Each one says, in one sentence, which part of the brief it serves and how. What the brief leaves out as to confirm is a guess: no source rests on it. When it leaves out who uses the app, start from what it is for and its loop. Find new ones each time, for this product.
2. **Pick six** that are far apart in colour, type, density and material. Drop any source whose obvious rendering is one of the banned looks. Mix kinds of source: at most two printed documents (forms, labels, manuals, plans all end up as white paper and a technical sans), and at least two that are not on paper at all, such as a material, a place, a light, a sound turned visible, a gesture of the trade.
3. **Give each worker its source and its sentence.** It takes what makes the source recognisable (its colour system, its type, its layout conventions, its marks and codes) and turns that into a web kit. It does not draw a picture of the object.

For a shop selling lamps, chairs and rugs, a first run picked a paint chip fan deck, the European energy label, an assembly sheet, a carbon-copy order form, a furnished floor plan and a 1975 mail-order catalogue. Five of the six were printed documents, and five came out as white paper with a technical sans: only the catalogue stood apart. Sources like the glow of a lampshade at dusk, a wool rug's dyed yarn on the loom, or a chair maker's bench with its oiled wood would have spread the six further. These are examples of the kind, not a list to reuse.

## For a game or a creative product

Players do not live in the real world of the things a game names: they live in its fiction and in its genre. A strategy game about explorers and wild beasts is not a hiking trail, a survey map or a camping tent, even though explorers walk trails and read maps. A first run on such a game drew six directions from the real outdoors (trail markers, undergrowth, a topographic map, a bivouac tent, a decorated cave, morning mist); the map won every duel, and the game came out looking like a printed survey sheet, with its painted beasts pasted onto it.

1. **List twelve sources from four kinds, at least two of each**, each with its sentence naming the part of the brief it serves:
   - **the fiction**: a place, a people, a ritual, an object of that world as the game tells it (the chief's hut at dusk, the totems of a tribe, a tamer's camp, a bestiary kept by the tribe);
   - **the illustration style of the visual assets**: their palette, their light, their finish, their outline, taken further into a whole interface;
   - **the interface conventions of the genre**: how the games of its references show resources, collections, inventories, cards, maps and timers, and how they put the interface inside the world (a counter carved in wood, a card framed like the beast's portrait);
   - **the materials and lights of that world**: what its things are made of and how they are lit, as the illustrations show it (oiled wood and leather, firelight, golden grass at sunset).

   These are examples of the kind, not a list to reuse: find the sources of this game, in its own brief.
2. **The visual assets are a constraint, not a source among others.** Every direction must make the existing illustrations and logo look at home: same family of light and colour, frames that suit them, nothing that makes them look pasted on. A direction that would clash with them is dropped, however good it looks alone. Name in each sentence what the direction keeps of them.
3. **Pick six** far apart in colour, type, density and material, as for a tool, with at most two from the same kind. Drop any whose obvious rendering is a banned look, the document interface first.
4. **Give each worker its source, its sentence, and the visual assets** as the brief lists them, with the paths of a few to look at: it opens them before it builds.

## Saying a direction in plain words

The person chooses between directions they may never have seen named. Each direction comes back from its worker with two plain sentences: what one sees (the colours, the materials, the light, the shapes of the blocks and buttons) and what it takes from the product, in the words of someone who is not a designer. No trade words: not « neatline », « graduated frame », « bento », « brutalist », « grotesque », « editorial ». « A map border of black and white squares around each block » says what « a graduated frame » hides.
